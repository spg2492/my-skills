# Article Template

Write every section as structured, polished prose. Do not use bullet points or lists. Each section should read as a coherent, publication-ready paragraph or set of paragraphs. The only exception is "How to Use It", which uses a numbered sequence where the flow requires it.

**Audience:** This article serves two readers simultaneously — a customer self-serving in a help center, and a CS, Sales, or Marketing team member using it to understand, explain, and position the feature in a customer conversation. Write it so a CS rep can hand it directly to a customer AND use it themselves to articulate the feature's value with confidence.

### Overview

Open with the specific customer problem this feature solves — make it concrete and recognizable, not abstract. Name the friction, inefficiency, or missed opportunity customers have been living with. Then pivot to the outcome: what can they now achieve that they couldn't before, and what does that mean for their pipeline, buyer experience, or team efficiency? Identify who will benefit most, framed in terms of their goals. The reader should finish this section feeling that this feature was built for them and understanding exactly what it unlocks — not just what it is. Do not describe functionality here — describe impact.

---

### Benefits

Make the business case explicitly and specifically. Name the concrete outcomes customers gain — faster pipeline progression, higher content engagement, reduced manual effort for marketing ops, better visibility into buyer intent, or improved rep efficiency. Go beyond "this saves time" and say what that time is worth: what can teams now do that they couldn't, and how does that connect to revenue or growth? Connect each capability to a real customer goal. Where PathFactory has proof points, benchmarks, or customer results in pathfactory-context.md that are relevant, weave them in as evidence — not as footnotes, but as part of the narrative. Do not describe features or list capabilities — write outcome-focused prose that makes a customer feel the value before they've used the feature.

---

### Prerequisites

*Include this section only if requirements exist. If there are no prerequisites, omit it entirely.*

Outline any requirements that must be met before using the feature. Cover permission requirements, necessary configurations, dependencies on other features, or integration setup. Ensure customers understand any setup needed before they begin.

### How It Works

Provide a conceptual explanation of the feature's behavior. Describe the logic behind the feature and how different components interact. For each key behavior or mechanism, briefly connect it to the customer benefit it enables — don't just explain what the system does, explain why that design choice is good for the customer. Help customers understand what happens when they use the feature and what outputs they can expect. Include a short narrative describing the flow from input to output, building confidence before customers begin using the feature. Do not include step-by-step instructions here.

---

### How to Use It

Open with one sentence that reminds the reader what they're about to accomplish — anchor the workflow in the outcome before the first step. Then walk customers through the primary workflow in a logical order, referencing relevant areas of the interface and explaining what to do at each step. At key decision points or high-value steps, briefly note why that action matters or what it unlocks — not on every step, but where it reinforces the value of the feature. Use a numbered sequence where the order of steps is essential. Where screenshots were captured, reference them inline as [Screenshot: filename] so they can be inserted into WordPress at the corresponding step.

---

### Example Use Cases

Demonstrate 2–3 practical scenarios where the feature provides value. Each scenario should reflect a realistic workflow a PathFactory customer would recognize — a specific team, goal, or situation. Show the feature in action, then close each scenario with the outcome achieved: what improved, what became possible, or what problem was eliminated. The ending of each scenario should make the reader think "that's exactly our situation." Write each scenario as a short, concrete narrative — not a hypothetical or abstract example.

---

### Getting the Most Out of [Feature Name]

Highlight 2–3 best practice tips that help customers get maximum value from the feature. Include guidance on configuration choices, common pitfalls to avoid, and how to apply the feature most effectively in their workflows. Where relevant, briefly connect back to outcomes or benefits mentioned earlier in the article to reinforce the value. Write as direct, empowering prose — not warnings or caveats, but practical guidance that helps customers succeed faster.

---

### FAQs

Address 3–5 questions customers are likely to ask or should know the answer to when encountering this feature for the first time. Derive these from the feature's behavior, configuration options, permissions, known limitations, and anything that might cause confusion or uncertainty. Do not repeat content already explained in full elsewhere in the article — if a question is covered in depth above, answer it briefly here and point back to the relevant section. Write each answer as a concise, informative paragraph that gives the customer confidence and reduces the need to contact support.

---

### Availability

Explain how customers can access the feature. Cover release status, plan or tier availability, role-based permissions, or any regional considerations. Ensure customers understand whether the feature is available in their environment and who can use it.

---

### Get Started

Close the article with a clear, action-oriented next step. Tell the customer exactly what to do now — navigate to the feature, reach out to their Customer Success Manager, attend an enablement session, or explore a related resource. Write 2–3 confident sentences that leave the reader with momentum, not just information.

## Tone Rules

- Write for B2B marketers and sales professionals, not engineers
- Use "you" language ("you can now...", "your team...", "this gives you...")
- Lead with outcomes, not features
- Benefits are not a section — they are a thread. Weave customer value into Overview, How It Works, How to Use It, and Example Use Cases. The Benefits section deepens it; the rest of the article reinforces it
- Be consultative — help the reader understand when and why to use this, not just how
- Use the natural language of PathFactory's audience: demand gen, buyer journey, content engagement, pipeline acceleration, target accounts, buying signals, content performance, ABM. These are not jargon — they are the vocabulary your readers use every day
- No technical jargon: no "payload", "orchestrator", "merge", "sprint", "QA"
- Confident and direct — avoid hedging language like "may", "might", "could potentially"
- Every section must read as polished, publication-ready prose
- Write as a senior PM who understands the customer's business: connect features to pipeline, revenue, efficiency, and buyer experience — not just to UI interactions
- Write with full depth and substance — never shorten a section to hit a length target. If a workflow has 10 steps, document all 10. If a concept needs 3 paragraphs to explain correctly, write 3 paragraphs
- Avoid re-explaining something at full length in a second section. Brief callbacks to earlier points are good writing — they maintain narrative flow and reinforce key messages. Verbatim repetition is not
- Where context is needed that lives in separate documentation, reference and link rather than re-explain
